Who profits from species extinction?

As OCES expands Project EROS with luxury sponsorship, critics say public conservation money is being redirected into branding, designer partnerships and media spectacle rather than habitat repair.

By Eleanor Marsh, Environment Correspondent3 October 2095

Summary

  • Campaigners question whether public funding and corporate sponsorship are changing conservation priorities.
  • The newly announced Lush partnership offers a visible case study in the commercial value of the emergency.

Public funds intended for the protection of endangered species are increasingly being channelled into luxury-brand collaborations, high-profile campaign launches and experimental reproductive design technologies, raising urgent questions about whether extinction has become a profitable market, according to critics and leaked documents.

OCES this week unveiled an expanded phase of its flagship initiative, Project EROS, complete with a roster of designer partners and a global media campaign, despite ongoing concerns about the limited progress of on-the-ground habitat restoration.

The programme was created in response to the Silent Sky Emergency, when declining bird populations began to affect food systems and everyday life. Its founding problem was ecological. Its increasingly public solution now also has a commercial identity: wearable prototypes, collaborations, launch events and a language borrowed from luxury design.

The value of being seen to care

For a sponsor, an endangered species offers more than a cause to support. It offers a recognisable image, a story of urgency and the possibility of associating a product with rescue. Campaigners argue that those benefits should be acknowledged when a partnership is presented as an uncomplicated act of care.

The newly announced Lush collaboration illustrates that tension. The partnership page openly presents Project EROS Phase II as supported by Lush, linking formulation and material expertise to research on scent and courtship signals. A co-branded collection gives the association a visible consumer form.

That page also makes a distinction easily lost in a promotional campaign: existing Orange-fronted Parakeet Bird Lingerie outcomes predate the partnership, and Phase II has no published recovery or courtship-success figures. The commercial relationship has a public presence before its ecological results can be assessed.

The campaign can be a success for a brand long before it is a success for a bird.

Following the priorities

Critics are asking for spending to be separated into categories that a reader can understand: field restoration, veterinary care, research, equipment, communications and commercial collaboration. A combined programme total cannot show whether habitats are receiving sustained support or whether public money is supporting activities that primarily benefit sponsors.

The documents cited by opponents have also prompted questions about how institutional success is measured. A rise in total breeding numbers can be an attractive headline while a particular species remains in decline. The same is true of a busy launch schedule: visibility is not evidence of ecological recovery.

The financial pressure can extend beyond OCES. Smaller conservation teams may be unable to obtain advanced equipment, while well-funded partnership projects can offer designers the budgets and publicity that attract further investment. An emergency system could develop a hierarchy in which the best-supported projects become easier to promote and therefore easier to fund again.

Sponsorship is not an answer to accountability

Corporate funding can contribute resources and specialist knowledge. The unanswered question is who controls the brief, the presentation of results and the terms on which a species becomes part of a brand story. Critics say independent welfare decisions and field priorities must remain insulated from the commercial timetable.

They are calling for partnership agreements, conflicts of interest and outcome measures to be published in a form that distinguishes promises from results. In the Lush case, they also want the new conservation narrative examined alongside concerns about the company’s earlier use of beech-forest resources and the food supply of Orange-fronted Parakeets.

The most difficult accounting may ultimately be ecological. How much viable habitat has been repaired? Which distinct species are recovering? How dependent have animals become on repeated interventions? Those questions cannot be answered by sales, coverage or the number of names on a sponsor wall.

As conservation becomes a marketable public identity, the beneficiaries are increasingly easy to see. Whether endangered birds share in that benefit remains the measure that matters.
