OCES accused of creating ‘breeding value scores’ for endangered birds

Campaigners say the ranking system reduces vulnerable species to reproductive data points and raises urgent ethical concerns.

By Science and Environment Desk8 September 2095

Summary

  • Campaigners question a system that ranks endangered birds by predicted reproductive potential.
  • Critics want OCES to explain how scores affect access to care, funding and breeding programmes.

OCES is facing growing criticism after it emerged that the organisation has developed a system that assigns “breeding value scores” to endangered birds, ranking them according to their perceived reproductive potential.

The scores form part of the monitoring infrastructure around Project EROS, the emergency programme that uses species-specific wearable devices to restore weakened courtship signals. Campaigners say a tool presented as a way to organise research could become a way to decide which animals deserve scarce resources.

The controversy centres on what happens after a number appears beside a bird’s record. A forecast of pairing success is not the same as a measure of ecological importance, welfare or the value of keeping a distinct species alive. Opponents fear those differences could disappear when funding decisions are made.

A forecast can become a decision

Conservation teams already need information about age, health, genetic diversity and the conditions in which an animal lives. The objection, critics say, is not to collecting useful information. It is to compressing uncertain and sometimes incompatible observations into a single ranking that appears more definitive than the evidence behind it.

A bird with weakened plumage or limited access to food could receive a poor prediction precisely because its habitat has deteriorated. If that prediction then reduces its access to support, the score would reinforce the damage it was supposed to help address. Campaigners want habitat conditions recorded separately from an individual animal’s reproductive potential.

A prediction of breeding success cannot answer the question of which lives deserve protection.

There is also a problem of comparison. The Great Tit, Atlantic Puffin and Golden-headed Quetzal communicate attraction in different ways. A single scale can conceal those differences, particularly when observations come from unequal research budgets or from animals held in captivity rather than living in the wild.

Who gets the next intervention?

The Front for Reproductive Ethics and Ecological Autonomy, known as FREE, is calling for an account of how rankings are used inside OCES. Its concerns include whether low-scoring animals can be excluded from advanced Bird Lingerie trials and whether sponsors can favour subjects that offer a more visible story of success.

Those questions have become sharper as Project EROS attracts fashion, cosmetics and lifestyle funding. A programme rewarded for rapid increases in pairing or hatching may prioritise animals likely to produce a favourable result, while more difficult cases remain out of view. A rising headline total would not necessarily show that the most vulnerable species were recovering.

Supporters of reproductive monitoring argue that limited staff and equipment make some form of prioritisation unavoidable. But a useful research tool needs clearly stated limits: the uncertainty around each prediction, the evidence used, the decisions it can inform and the decisions it must never make alone.

The questions OCES must answer

Campaigners want independent scrutiny of the scoring method, a record of decisions influenced by it and a route for field teams to challenge a ranking. They also want welfare needs protected regardless of score, and species recovery assessed through genetic diversity, viable wild populations and habitat quality rather than reproductive totals alone.

The published partnership material describes species-specific research and independent welfare review, but does not explain a breeding-value ranking system. That gap matters: a general promise of responsible conservation is not an account of how a particular animal is assessed or treated.

Until the methodology and its consequences are visible, the dispute remains larger than a table of numbers. It is about whether emergency conservation is being organised around the needs of endangered birds, or around the results an institution can most easily count.
