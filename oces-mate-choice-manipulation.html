Science / Project EROS

Concerns grow over OCES project accused of manipulating birds’ mate choice

The debate over Project EROS asks whether restoring courtship signals has become a programme for designing attraction itself.

By Science Correspondent29 September 2095

Summary

  • Critics say Project EROS is shaping attraction signals rather than restoring habitats.
  • Campaigners argue endangered birds are being pushed into engineered mating behaviours.

Critics of OCES say its flagship Project EROS has moved beyond conservation into the design of courtship itself. The scheme, originally presented as a response to collapsing bird populations, is now facing scrutiny over whether it artificially alters colour, display and attraction cues in endangered species.

Campaigners and independent ecologists argue that the programme reflects a wider failure to address habitat loss, insect decline and environmental degradation. OCES says the project is intended to support reproduction, but opponents claim the line between conservation and control is becoming increasingly difficult to defend.

Restoring a signal, or changing the choice?

The original problem is straightforward to describe and difficult to solve. Birds can remain physically capable of breeding while pollution, poor nutrition and fragmented habitats weaken the signals used to recognise and attract a mate. For a Great Tit, that can include the yellow breast plumage associated with condition and food quality.

Project EROS attempts to intervene through species-specific Bird Lingerie. The ethical question is whether an artificial colour or surface merely replaces a signal that environmental damage has obscured, or presents a new signal that changes which partner another bird would select.

Those are not interchangeable outcomes. A device that improves the appearance of health without improving the conditions behind it could disconnect an attraction cue from the information it once carried. More pairings would not, by themselves, demonstrate that the intervention had restored a functioning ecological relationship.

LOVE IS NOT CONSERVATION TECHNOLOGY.

The slogan comes from FREE, the Front for Reproductive Ethics and Ecological Autonomy. The group argues that animal choice should be treated as part of conservation, rather than an obstacle to a reproductive target. Its central challenge is directed at a system that can measure the result of a pairing more easily than the freedom behind it.

A new phase brings new questions

The Lush–OCES partnership page describes Phase II research into scent, material interaction and species-specific courtship signals. It says human fragrance expertise informs the work, while any avian application needs its own welfare assessment and evidence. It also states that Phase II has no published recovery or courtship-success figures yet.

For critics, those limitations should remain visible wherever the programme is promoted. A research question about scent is not evidence that a fragrance-derived intervention is safe or useful for birds. Findings from the Orange-fronted Parakeet cannot automatically justify the same approach for the Great Tit, Atlantic Puffin or Golden-headed Quetzal.

There are concerns about unintended pairings, changes in social behaviour and the possibility that successful-looking reproductive totals could conceal a loss of species identity. The risks would need to be assessed over time, including after wearable equipment is removed and beyond the controlled conditions of a trial.

The habitat question has not gone away

Even a technically successful intervention would leave the causes of the emergency intact if food, nesting sites and connected habitats continue to disappear. Campaigners say the programme must show how spending on attraction technologies relates to the slower work of repairing those conditions.

The dispute is therefore about more than whether an individual device works. It asks what counts as recovery: additional eggs, additional chicks, or self-sustaining populations capable of living and choosing mates without continual human redesign.

As Project EROS expands, that distinction may become its most consequential test. Keeping a bird alive and making it breed are measurable achievements. Whether the programme is preserving the life of a species is a harder question.
